01Long/short mechanics

It's a whole basket of stocks being longed and shorted at the same time. That's where the losses to harvest come from, regardless of which way the market goes.

Where I got stuck: I thought it was one pair

I was tunnel-visioned on this being a single pair of stocks. Pepsi against Coke, one bet, and I couldn't see how that produced enough losses to matter or why we'd be confident about it.

It isn't one pair. It's a whole basket of 400 to 600+ names depending on the account. In the sample Overlay 30/30 on slide 21, it's 200–300 long holdings and 200–300 short. A sample statement Alfred walked through on a $1M account showed 259 names long and 145 short on a $1M account.

Once it's a basket, the logic changes. It isn't a call on one name. Some go the right way, some don't, and there are enough positions in play that the aggregate produces losses. That was the piece that made it click for me.

Alfred's version

Alfred uses Bank of America against Wells Fargo to show how a single pair gets built inside the basket. Two banks, same sector, one longed and one shorted, so sector direction cancels. If the sector drops 3%, both fall, the long loses and the short gains, roughly a wash. The pair only pays if the long name outperforms the short name. Bank of America down 2.7% against Wells Fargo down 3% pockets 0.3%.

On how Quantinno picks, Alfred's framing: their algorithm and factor model decide what to go long and what to go short, based on their evaluation at the time. Slide 30 lists the factor categories (valuation, momentum, quality, sustainable earnings, management signaling, investor sentiment, behavioral bias) with 150+ underlying signals and 1,000+ stocks scored daily.

The other thing worth holding onto: positions don't stay put. Quantinno rescores continuously, so a name that's a long today can become a short in two years as fundamentals shift. There's real turnover, and some short-term gains get realized along the way. The bet is on them being right on average, not right on every name.

One client's own plain-English version, which Alfred confirmed was exactly right: borrow against the stock I already own on margin, and use that money to buy actual stock that goes long and short. Good phrasing to hear back from a client.

02Reading 130/30

For every $100 in the account: $130 of stock bought long, $30 of stock sold short. Net market exposure is $130 minus $30 = $100, the same as owning the market outright. Gross activity is $160.

The extra $30 long and $30 short run on margin at the custodian. Slide 40 breaks the cost down: the long side pays Fed funds plus a 0.5% spread on the extension, the short side earns a rebate at Fed funds minus 0.5%, netting to roughly 0.30% pre-tax, or about 0.18% after tax at a 40.8% marginal rate.

The reason to run 130/30 instead of 100/0 is that Quantinno now has $160 of stock to work with instead of $100. More positions in play, more opportunities to realize losses each year.

Other levels: 145/45, 175/75, 200/100. Each step is more aggressive. 130/30 is the default. Slide 28 also lists beta-adjusted variants (115/45 at 0.7 beta, 150/100 at 0.5 beta) if a client wants less market exposure than the index.

How the leverage level actually gets chosen

This was the thing I kept circling in the July 15 meeting: how do you decide someone should be at 200/100 versus something smaller, and how do you get comfortable pushing it.

Where I've landed: it's our call with the client, not something their holdings dictate, and it comes down to two things.

  • Can they stomach the tracking error? Slide 18 shows target tracking error going from 1.3–1.5% at 130/30 to 3–3.5% at 200/100. Target excess return roughly doubles too, from 0.6% to 1.4%. But tracking error cuts both ways. In a 4% market year you might be up 1%. The higher you go, the more room there is to be wrong.
  • Do they have gains elsewhere to absorb the losses? Higher leverage produces more losses. If there's nothing to apply them against, the extra tracking error buys nothing.

Practical constraint: anything above 145/45 requires portfolio margin at the custodian, which carries higher account minimums and more paperwork.

The 200/100 client is someone mid-liquidity event with a large balance sheet and ongoing taxable events, where this is a slice of a $20M–$40M picture. Bigger risk for bigger benefit, knowing it won't hit every year. Similar mindset to a venture allocation.

When a client asks which leverage level, what are you defaulting to, and how are you explaining that more tax benefit also means more tracking error?

03Why losses keep coming when standard tax loss harvesting runs out

The way I think about this: the long side is a garden, the short side is a rented parking lot.

The long side is a garden. Quantinno plants roughly 500 names at the start. Winners grow and get held. Losers get harvested and replanted. After four or five years of a rising market, most positions sit well above cost basis and there's nothing left to sell at a loss. That's where standard direct-indexed tax loss harvesting stops producing anything meaningful. Slide 11 shows long-only dropping to about 1% by year 4 and effectively nothing after year 5.

The short side is a rented parking lot. Positions rotate constantly. Every time a shorted name rises against the position, Quantinno closes it at a loss and opens a new short somewhere else. Some stay a while, some turn over fast. That constant churn is why loss generation keeps going instead of tapering to zero. Slide 11 has 130/30 holding at 3% per year from year 6 onward.

The part I had to sit with: this isn't a clean machine where every short is a winner. Quantinno rescores names over time, so what starts as a long can become a short later. Real buying and selling, and some short-term gains realized in the process. The strategy is a bet that they're right on average with the rescoring, not perfect on any given name.

Alfred's number for the mature state: once the strategy has settled, you're looking at roughly 4% to 4.5% of account value per year in net capital losses, ongoing. Not much, but enough to keep offsetting gains on distributions or other events.

04Sources of return

Two things running at once, and they're not the same size.

Tax benefit is the main one. On a $10M account at 130/30, slide 18 puts the annual net tax benefit around $340,000, or 3.4% of market value. It's mechanical. It doesn't depend on Quantinno being right about which stocks to pick.

Alpha is the smaller piece. Quantinno picks longs it expects to outperform and shorts it expects to underperform. If they're right on average, that's another 0.6% at 130/30, up to 1.4% at 200/100. Real, but it's the boost, not the case.

Alfred's framing: the goal isn't necessarily to beat the market. The tax benefit is structural, and the economic outperformance is what shows up on top when it does.

When I frame this for a client, I lead with the tax benefit. Alpha is upside, not the pitch. Slide 36 backs that up. The pre-tax outperformance column is negative in plenty of months, but the tax benefit column is positive in every single one.

05Deferral and the unwind

The tax benefit is largely deferral, not elimination. Every dollar of realized loss creates a dollar of embedded gain elsewhere in the portfolio. When the client eventually unwinds, those gains get realized.

Alfred's line: the point of long/short isn't that we're getting rid of the taxes ever, we're delaying them.

What makes deferral worth something in real terms:

  • Paying tax later is worth less than paying it today.
  • The client may be in a lower bracket later, whether from retirement or a move to a no-tax state.
  • Step-up in basis at death eliminates the deferred gain entirely.
  • The deferred tax dollars stay invested and compound in the meantime.

Alfred's 401(k) analogy is the cleanest way to say this to a client. Contribute $24,500 this year and delay the tax, then distribute it back out to yourself next year and pay the tax. You deferred one year and then paid it. No real benefit. Long/short works the same way. Use it for a year and unwind, and the benefit reverses.

What actually unwinds

Worth being precise here, because it changes the number. Quantinno removes the 30/30 extensions and returns the core "100" to the client (slide 43). The direct-indexed middle doesn't get liquidated. So the tax cost at unwind comes from the extensions, not the whole account.

The cost lands in three buckets:

  1. Long-term capital gains from the long extension. The primary source, since gains embed as the market appreciates.
  2. Short-term capital gains from the long extension. The smallest piece. Quantinno actively minimizes this because of the higher rate.
  3. Short-term capital gains from the short extension. Small embedded gain but large tax cost, because the short book turns over frequently and it's all short-term.

Alfred's summary: all of this equals whatever losses you took out. Take $165K in losses over the life of the strategy, and unwinding produces roughly $165K in gains across those three buckets. That's why the strategy has to run long enough for the deferral to earn its keep.

Commitment horizon

Alfred's guidance: four years minimum. The heavy tax loss harvesting is concentrated in the first four to five years and levels off after that, so exiting inside that window means paying back the benefit before you've had time to use it. Ten years is where he'd rather be, then a gradual unwind over five.

Slide 44 contrasts an immediate unwind (extensions removed at T+1, full capital gains bill in one year) against reducing leverage over four years and spreading the bill across tax years. Same total, very different tax outcome.

Alfred wants this said before a client commits, because it determines sizing. Whatever they want for liquidity or freedom stays outside the strategy. Someone with $5M in a single name shouldn't put all $5M in.

06Core, Exchange, and Overlay

Same underlying engine, deployed three ways depending on what the client brings. I'm going through these in the order they made sense to me rather than the order they appear in the deck, because two of them are straightforward and one took work.

DEALS Core: the direct indexing one

Client shows up with cash, or with a portfolio that can be rebuilt from scratch. Quantinno constructs the whole thing: the $100 direct-indexed core plus the $30 long and $30 short extensions. Losses report on the client's 1099 and their CPA applies them wherever.

Once I connected it to direct indexing it was easy. It's the same concept we already use, with the long/short bolted on. Alfred's version of the ideal case: someone hands us $1M in cash, we buy into a matched benchmark (say 70% US / 30% international), and every position in that middle is an individual stock. So Quantinno is harvesting across the core and the extensions, all year.

Ray made the connection in the July 22 meeting and it's the right one: this is the same reason cash is the best funding source for regular direct indexing. No embedded gains to work around. A client account sitting at 5% unrealized gain is close enough to a cash account that it can transition into Core without much friction.

DEALS Exchange: the tax exchange fund concept

Client has a concentrated low-basis position they want to diversify out of. Quantinno uses the losses from the long/short extensions to offset the tax bill from gradually selling it down, and reinvests the proceeds into a benchmark. Over several years the client transitions from concentrated to diversified at roughly tax-neutral cost.

This one reads to me like a tax exchange fund, and that's the shortcut I'd use with a client who knows what those are, with one difference worth being careful about. In a tax exchange fund, once you're exchanged, you're out of the exposure. Here you're not; the shares sell down over years.

The 1099 difference. In Core and Overlay, the losses hit the client's 1099 and the CPA can point them at anything: RSU sales, a rental property, whatever. In Exchange, the losses are pre-committed to offsetting the sale of the concentrated stock. They're consumed internally. The client technically still has loss activity, but it's more of a wash. They experience the benefit as tax-neutral diversification, not as a number they get to deploy.

DEALS Overlay: the one that took me longer

This is the one I needed an example for. Think QQQ, or an individual stock the client is bullish on long term. They're not trying to sell it. But it's sitting there doing nothing, so let's finesse some losses out of it.

Quantinno leaves the holding untouched and layers a $30 long extension and $30 short extension on top, using the existing position as margin collateral. No new funding required. The client keeps their exposure and gets loss generation alongside it.

Framing it as "the client refuses to sell" is wrong. It's not refusal, it's conviction. They want the position.

Why Overlay generates fewer losses than Core

Once the three definitions are clear, the loss numbers explain themselves. Cash-funded Core means more stocks in play, more to harvest across. Overlay means you're only working the margin extensions. The middle is off-limits.

Alfred put it directly: in Overlay, there's nothing you can do with the concentrated position in the middle. Quantinno can only harvest off the extensions.

Core at 145/45 generates 28.5% in year one (slide 19). Overlay at 45/45 generates 16.5% (slide 23). Similar-looking labels, different harvestable surface.

Dynamic hedging: an add-on to Exchange

Optional layer for a client worried about carrying concentrated risk during a multi-year sell-down. Alfred's framing maps it to something clients already understand: it works like a covered call, except instead of the client keeping the premium, Quantinno uses the premium to buy a protective put. Premium-neutral collar. Floor and ceiling on the position while the shares sell down over time.

His example: if the client likes where the stock is trading today, the collar locks that range in immediately, so concentration risk is effectively handled now even though the shares take years to physically diversify out. That's the answer for a client who wants out of the exposure but can't stomach the tax hit of liquidating.

Constraints worth knowing before offering it (slide 45): $3M account minimum and $3M per collared position, top-100 US large caps only, up to 5 stocks, collar must cover the full position. It can't be paused or adjusted once running. And it's incompatible with QSBS. Hedging before five years kills QSBS status entirely, and after five years you'd have to elect a deemed sale. Quantinno doesn't recommend it for QSBS stock at all.

Mixing Exchange and Overlay on the same position

Alfred walked through this on a live case and it's worth having ready. On a $4.5M concentrated position: run Exchange on $3.5M (actively diversifying over about three years) and Overlay on the remaining $1M (generating losses without diversifying it). The Overlay losses can be applied to the Exchange sales, potentially cutting a three-year timeline closer to two.

Tradeoff: using the Overlay losses that way lowers the cost basis of the remaining $1M of the position.

$1M minimum per strategy. Both sides of a mixed structure have to independently clear it, and capital is provisioned per strategy at inception. You can't casually shift dollars between them later.

07Fees

Alfred's explanation is the one to use here, so this section is his rather than mine.

The stack on a standard DEALS Core account:

  • Quantinno management fee: 0.45%
  • DiversiFi advisory fee: 0.6% to 0.8%, depending on account size
  • Financing on the margin running the extensions: roughly 0.15%
  • All-in: approximately 1.2%

That total is in the same range as our normal fee. So the incremental cost to the client versus a plain portfolio is essentially Quantinno's management fee, and what it buys is the tax loss harvesting engine, roughly $340K of annual net tax benefit on a $10M account at 130/30.

The comparison Alfred wants made explicit in the meeting: strategy fees and financing costs against tax benefit and long-term performance benefit. Ray framed it the same way. The client wants to know what they're saving on tax versus what they're paying on AUM. Say the math out loud rather than quoting a rate.

An important detail on slide 36

The net portfolio return column is net of Quantinno's 0.45% management fee and net of financing and transaction charges. It is not net of our advisory fee. At 0.8% annually that's about 0.06% a month coming off those figures. Not much against the tax benefit, but worth flagging before the client works it out themselves.

08Manager selection

Clients who've researched long/short will ask why Quantinno. Alfred's due diligence from the July 22 meeting:

AQR. Market leader, roughly $200B in long/short. Paused on new advisor relationships unless the firm has $5B+ in AUM. Minimum is $3M per strategy, so a client wanting both Exchange and Overlay needs $6M. Not accessible to us at our current size, and the minimums would exclude most of the client base anyway.

BlackRock. Around $5B in long/short. Newer to the space and likely to grow on brand, but not a proven specialist yet.

Quantinno. Roughly $80B in long/short and it's all they do. $1M minimum per strategy, so Exchange plus Overlay needs $2M rather than $6M. Structurally it resembles an AQR spinoff. Founder Hoon Kim ran portfolio construction in AQR's Global Stock Selection Group, and most of the executive team came from there. They've been good to work with.

Custodian constraints are worth knowing too. Fidelity has paused new RIAs joining long/short. Schwab caps long/short at 20–30% of firm AUM, so a firm with $100M can only place $30M. The leverage is why.

09Anticipated client questions

The ones I expect to come up most, and how I'd answer them today.

What are the fees?

About 1.2% all-in, which is roughly our normal fee. See Section 07.

How do the losses actually help me?

Depends which strategy. In Core and Overlay they show up on the 1099 and the CPA applies them against gains elsewhere. In Exchange they're consumed internally offsetting the sale of the concentrated stock.

Can I see real performance, not projections?

Yes. Slide 36 is composite performance of actual DEALS Core 130/30 accounts from March 2021 through June 2026. Inception to date: 123.5% net portfolio return against 112.5% for the S&P 500, 11.0% pre-tax outperformance, 27.4% tax benefit, 38.4% cumulative after-tax outperformance. On a $1M account, that 27.4% tax benefit is $274K in losses. Growth of $1M over the period: $2.23M in the strategy versus $2.13M in the S&P. Two caveats: those are DEALS Core specifically, not Overlay or Exchange, and they're net of Quantinno's fee but not ours.

How long do I need to stay in?

Alfred: four years minimum, ideally ten, then unwind gradually over about five.

What if I want out early?

You can, but the gains realized at unwind offset the losses you took. Quantinno returns the core portfolio intact; the extensions are what produce the gain.

What if the market drops?

Net market exposure matches owning the index, so the account falls with the market. Slide 21: the extensions are built market-neutral in sector and factor exposure, so in a downturn losses on the long extensions should be largely offset by gains on the shorts.

When do we decide to sell my concentrated position?

Alfred's framing: if there were zero tax consequences to exchanging out today, how much would you do? Whatever they answer becomes the target. Then Exchange on the portion they want gone, Overlay or nothing on the portion they want to keep.

Can I use the losses against something specific, like a property sale?

In Core and Overlay, yes. In Exchange they're already committed to the stock sales, though Alfred told a client it's possible to redirect them, it just delays the diversification. The cleaner answer is running Overlay on a separate portion for that purpose. Alfred's line: the capital loss is just a number on your tax return, how you use it is up to you.

This came up on a live client call. The client had rental properties they were planning to sell. Worth expecting it.

Do I meet with Quantinno?

No. Everything comes through us. We hold the statements, we walk you through performance and tax activity, we handle anything that needs to go back to them. Quantinno is the engine, not a relationship you manage.

How is the fee paid?

Automatically from the Schwab account, same as everything else. No invoicing.

10Client fit

Alfred's criteria from the July 22 meeting: concentrated stock with low cost basis, or cash, or anyone who can see themselves realizing high capital gains. That last one is broad on purpose. The gains could come from a property sale, a liquidity event, or from diversifying the stock itself.

Hard constraints

  • $1M minimum per strategy. $2M if they want Exchange and Overlay both.
  • Dynamic hedging is $3M per account and $3M per collared position, top-100 US large caps only.
  • Above 145/45 requires portfolio margin at the custodian.
  • Four years minimum in the strategy, per Alfred. Ten is where he'd rather be.

Where it gets harder to call

On July 15 I raised a client retiring next year and whether an aggressive version makes sense there. Alfred's answer was that it can, but the money isn't needed, so the conservative end is the better fit. That's the pattern: retiring doesn't rule it out, it moves you down the leverage scale.

Two things I'd want a real answer on before recommending it to someone drawing down: whether they have gains coming from anywhere else, and whether they can sit through a year where tracking error works against them.

12Vocabulary and open questions

Vocabulary

130/30 (or 145/45, 175/75, 200/100). Dollar exposure notation. For every $100 in the account: $130 long, $30 short. Net market exposure $100, gross activity $160. The extra long and short run on margin.

Direct indexing. Holding the individual stocks that make up an index rather than the index ETF, so each position carries its own cost basis and can be acted on separately for tax purposes.

Long extension. The additional long positions above the 100% core, funded on margin.

Short extension. Borrowed stock sold short. Produces a loss when closed at a higher price than it was opened.

Tracking error. How far the portfolio's return can drift from the benchmark, expressed as a standard deviation. Higher leverage means higher tracking error in both directions.

Embedded gain. Unrealized gain sitting between current market value and cost basis. Becomes taxable at sale or unwind.

Options collar / dynamic hedging. A protective put plus a covered call, structured to be premium-neutral, putting a floor and ceiling on a concentrated position while it diversifies down.

Assignment risk. On a collar, the risk that the stock rallies past the short call strike and gets called away, forcing sale of the full position and taxable gains. Quantinno monitors intraday to minimize it.
